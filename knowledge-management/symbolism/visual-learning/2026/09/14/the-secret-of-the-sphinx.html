Imagine losing every map you know. You can still see the sky—and, ahead of you, the Sphinx and three pyramids. Could they help you find your bearings?

This article explores the monuments as a symbolic compass for future humanity: a way to remember natural cycles after a civilization loses its knowledge. Anubis is central to that reading: the Sphinx–Anubis marks the threshold where the lost traveler begins, and where the traveler returns.

At a Glance

Follow four connected ideas: cycles → celestial signs → monuments → a new beginning. Astronomy supplies the observable patterns; Egyptian figures and later traditions supply symbolic connections.

As Above, So Below

Think of sunrise: a visible sign that helps you plan your day. As above, so below extends that idea—patterns in the sky can help us organize life on Earth.

Daylight, seasons and returning stars offered practical cues for travel and cultivation. Recognizing a cycle lets you prepare for its return.

The Three Cycles

A day is easy to notice. A year takes memory. A cycle lasting thousands of years needs knowledge passed between generations.

Here is the proposed mapping. The pyramid associations are symbolic, not established accounts of their original purpose.

Cycle Observable marker Pyramid and symbolic figure
Day and night Sunrise and sunset Menkaure · Horus
Seasons Annual return; Sirius’s heliacal rising was an Egyptian seasonal marker Khafre · Thoth–Sopdet
Great Year Earth’s slow axial precession against the stars Khufu · Osiris–Orion

The memory cue is larger monument, longer cycle. Sirius marks seasonal return; it can also serve as a reference against the changing precessional frame. It does not itself move one degree every 72 years.

The Three Cycles as Stages of Consciousness

The same pattern can describe widening awareness:

  • Daily cycle: the Initiate. Immediate experience brings questions of identity, desire and conflict: Who am I?
  • Annual cycle: life and transformation. Seasons teach impermanence, death and renewal. Awareness grows beyond the isolated self.
  • Great cycle: collective consciousness. The horizon becomes humanity across civilizations: What are we becoming together?

These are interpretive stages. The long astronomical cycle becomes a way to think about memory and responsibility beyond one lifetime.

Sirius: A Sign of Return

Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, belongs to Canis Major, the Great Dog. Its heliacal rising is its first visible appearance before sunrise after a period of absence. In ancient Egypt, that return was an important seasonal sign associated with the Nile and the new year.

The star does not create the cycle; it makes the return recognizable.

In this reading, Sopdet, the Egyptian personification of Sirius, joins Thoth, associated here with knowledge and perception. Thoth–Sopdet connects seasonal return with awakening. Orion, associated with Osiris in Egyptian religion, supplies the figure for the longer path of transformation.

The Dog Star offers a memorable connection to Anubis. At its heliacal rising, Sirius appears before the Sun: symbolically, a celestial dog at the threshold of returning light. Sirius marks the way above; Anubis marks passage below. This is a symbolic correspondence, not a claim that Sirius accompanies the Sun every day.

When the Traveler Loses the Way

Imagine a traveler lost in the desert. Familiar landmarks are gone. In this imagined scene, jackal tracks suggest a route the traveler cannot yet see: the animal knows the territory.

That story gives Anubis a guiding role through instinct, protection and passage. In the Papyrus of Hunefer, Anubis appears at the beginning of a decisive funerary passage. The jackal story helps us picture why such a guide matters when familiar bearings fail.

Here, however, Anubis is the Sphinx itself, a stationary threshold. The traveler reaches it alone. Sphinx–Anubis opens the way through all three pyramids and their cycles, then marks the place of return. Its role holds the whole compass together.

Robert and Olivia Temple propose a related modern hypothesis in The Sphinx Mystery: The Forgotten Origins of the Sanctuary of Anubis: an originally Anubis-shaped Sphinx within a water-filled enclosure linked to the Pyramid Texts’ “Jackal Lake.” This is a hypothesis, not an established identification; the surviving Sphinx has a human head and a lion’s body.

Now enlarge the desert story: after a catastrophe, humanity could become that traveler. With its old paths lost, it would need enduring signs to recover orientation. In this interpretation, Sphinx–Anubis is where that recovery begins.

The Sky Becomes a Compass

Look upward: Orion–Osiris and Sirius supply celestial signs. Look downward: the pyramids give the proposed cycles a durable form. Sphinx–Anubis is the entrance and return, connecting the traveler with this landscape.

Herodotus’s Histories, Book II, describes causeways and subterranean works associated with the Giza monuments. His account adds historical context to the remembered landscape; it does not establish this symbolic compass.

Pause and try it: if Sirius helps you recognize seasonal return, which pyramid represents that cycle here? Khafre, through Thoth–Sopdet. Anubis supplies the threshold into that path, as into the other two.

The Traveler, Zero and Twenty-Two

The Tarot Fool offers a later analogy: a traveler, a small dog and a journey about to begin. The dog can suggest instinct, warning and guidance—an echo of the jackal story, not evidence of an ancient Egyptian Tarot system.

Traditionally numbered 0, the Fool can also be imagined after 21, beginning again. In this symbolic 0/22 reading, the traveler stands at both entrance and return.

The three paths give that journey a rhythm:

  • Osiris–Orion · Khufu · Transformation: descent, loss and recovery of memory; the Great Year.
  • Thoth–Sopdet · Khafre · Awakening: observation, knowledge and recognition of return; the annual cycle.
  • Horus · Menkaure · Experience: renewed vision and order carried into life; the daily cycle.

Anubis needs no fourth path. Sphinx–Anubis permits passage into all three and receives the traveler again. The Fool represents humanity’s openness to begin anew; Anubis marks the enduring threshold.

The Great Year

Earth’s axis slowly changes direction through axial precession, completing a cycle in approximately 26,000 years. The rounded estimate of one degree per 72 years gives about 25,920 years for a full circuit.

Could civilizations have long “seasons” too? That is a separate question. Hindu Yugas, classical metal ages and astrological ages offer distinct cultural systems, not interchangeable astronomical measurements.

Sri Yukteswar proposed a 24,000-year spiritual cycle of ascent and descent. In Yuga language, Kali Yuga describes a dark phase of diminished awareness and material limitation.

Walter Cruttenden’s Lost Star of Myth and Time and The Great Year explore recurring ages and a companion-star hypothesis. Keep that proposed physical explanation distinct from established axial precession.

A Legacy for Future Humanity

What would you leave for people who no longer understood your language?

Books and institutions are fragile. In this interpretation, immense monuments could carry a memory through abandonment and catastrophe. Day and night can be rediscovered in a day; seasons take a year. Remembering the Great Year would require a legacy across generations.

The traveler is future humanity, searching for bearings after a rupture. Sphinx–Anubis marks the threshold: look upward, recognize the cycles, then explore their proposed reflection on Earth.

Perhaps this was the intended legacy—a compass through time. That purpose remains an interpretation, but its question is useful today: what knowledge should survive us, and how will someone find the way back to it?
